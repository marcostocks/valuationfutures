Valuation Futures — Protocol Overview

Valuation Futures are event-driven derivatives that price a single, verifiable outcome: at what total valuation will the company list its IPO? Rather than forcing continuous per-share pricing onto an illiquid private asset, each contract references the issuer's total enterprise valuation, trades 24/7, and on listing automatically rebases to per-share units and cash-settles against the IPO first-day close.

Dilution-Proof Valuation Pricing

Private companies raise across multiple rounds with different share classes, liquidation preferences, and anti-dilution terms. A per-share futures contract breaks every time a new round closes — its reference and economic exposure shift. Marco prices the total valuation instead:

Total Valuation  =  Share Price × Fully-Diluted Shares Outstanding

A $30B contract remains a $30B contract regardless of interim funding rounds, new share classes, option-pool increases, or anti-dilution adjustments. Only the IPO prospectus determines the final conversion to per-share units, so the contract is immune to dilution across the entire pre-IPO period.

The Mark Price

Private equity has no continuous, arbitrageable spot market, so the mark cannot be tied to a single live feed. Marco derives it from two sources — a fast, permissionless internal signal and a slow, verifiable external anchor — and combines them by a simple rule: the mark is internal by default, external by override.

Internal — what traders are paying. The internal mark is derived entirely from Marco's own order book. It is the market's continuous, real-time opinion of the company's valuation, reflecting sentiment, rumour, and positioning; it is fast, responsive, and entirely permissionless.

  • Construction. Executed trades feed a volume-weighted median over a rolling window (4–24h), smoothed into an 8-hour EMA to damp single-print noise and raise the cost of manipulation.
  • Unique-trader ratio. A minimum share of distinct buyers and sellers must be active in the window for the signal to qualify.
  • Median order size. Dust trades are excluded, so a spray of tiny fills cannot walk the mark.
  • Outlier rejection. Prints that deviate sharply from the current EMA are ignored unless independently confirmed.
  • Thin-book freeze. If activity collapses or the book concentrates in a few addresses, the EMA holds at its last valid value until healthy two-sided flow resumes.

External — what the company is verifiably worth. The external anchor is the last hard, discrete transaction in the target's equity — the price at which an informed buyer and seller actually exchanged value. It is slow, sparse, and indisputable, and it does not drift between events.

  • Qualifying events. A confirmed primary funding round with a stated post-money valuation, a secondary block trade above a minimum size, or a corporate action with a clear valuation implication.
  • Event-driven only. Between events the anchor is fixed; it moves solely when a new event is confirmed by multiple independent sources.
  • Convergence, not a jump. On a confirmed event the mark converges to the new anchor over a 24–48h window rather than instantly, preventing liquidation cascades.

How they combine. In normal operation the internal EMA is the mark and the market trades freely — the mark reflects the market. When a hard external event is confirmed, the mark converges to the new anchor over the override window and the internal EMA is set aside until convergence completes. At expiry the contract settles on the last verified external anchor — a funding round, a qualifying secondary, a corporate action, and ultimately the IPO first-day close (see The Rebase Mechanism) — regardless of where the internal mark sits.

The design therefore carries two truths: the trading truth — what the market believes now — and the settlement truth — what the company was verifiably worth at a specific moment. The gap between them is where traders take positions, and that gap is the entire point of the market.

The Rebase Mechanism

The IPO is the terminal external event. When the company files its final public prospectus (Form F-1 / S-1 or equivalent), the contract executes an automated, PnL-neutral rebase from valuation points to per-share units. The prospectus is the single source of truth for the conversion — mechanical, not discretionary.

Shares Per Contract  =  $1B ÷ IPO Price

Worked example. A prospectus discloses 200,000,000 fully-diluted shares at a $45–$50 range (midpoint $47.50), implying a $9.5B valuation. Each 1-point ($1B) contract converts to $1B ÷ $47.50 ≈ 21,052,631 shares, so a 9.5-contract position maps to 200,000,000 shares with no change in mark-to-market value at the instant of rebase. If the stock then closes its first public day at $52.00, each contract is worth 21,052,631 × $52.00 ≈ $1.0947B — a PnL of +$94.7M per contract versus the $1.0B baseline.

Settlement is thus clean and verifiable: the rebase is triggered by a public filing, and the payout references the IPO first-day closing price on the listing exchange (e.g. HKEX or the STAR Market).

Settlement & Fallback Logic

The contracts include explicit fallback logic for non-standard outcomes:

IPO — standard
Rebase at prospectus; settle against the IPO first-day close.
IPO delayed > 24 months
Converts to NAV-based settlement via three independent valuation firms.
M&A / acquisition
Settles at the acquisition enterprise value (publicly disclosed).
Direct listing
Uses the reference price (not the opening trade) as settlement.
Company withdraws IPO
Contract voided; initial margin returned with no PnL.

The displayed countdown is the expected IPO date and is indicative only; settlement is contingent on the realised event and resolved by the rules above.

The Liquidity Flywheel

Pure prediction venues suffer one-sided flow: when a private AI name generates hype, retail floods the long side and the platform must subsidise market-makers to take the short — economically unsustainable. Marco instead attracts a diverse ecosystem of natural short sellers to absorb retail long demand:

  • Investors with locked long positions — holders of real but still-illiquid pre-IPO equity — short Valuation Futures to lock in gains they cannot yet realise.
  • Institutional market-makers capture attractive long-funding yields delta-neutrally.
  • VCs, founders, and external holders proxy-hedge concentrated off-platform positions.

As natural short depth grows, spreads tighten, institutional adoption accelerates, and retail confidence rises — drawing in more such investors and, in turn, more natural short supply. Each participant's activity benefits the others, producing a self-sustaining liquidity flywheel rather than a subsidised book. Contracts are backed 1:1 by real, locked pre-IPO equity.

Institutional Use Cases

  • Investor with a locked long position — hedge downside before the IPO by shorting futures against illiquid pre-IPO holdings.
  • VC firm — lock in a portfolio mark without selling, using futures as a proxy hedge for off-platform positions.
  • Hedge fund — relative value: long an undervalued private name, short its public comparables.
  • Market-maker — capture funding-rate yield via delta-neutral long/short pairs.
  • Retail trader — express an IPO-valuation view with low capital (10% margin).
  • Family office — diversify pre-IPO exposure across spot equity and derivatives.

Margin & Liquidation

Positions use isolated margin: collateral is segregated to a single position, so a loss cannot propagate across markets. Initial margin can be as low as 10% (up to 10× leverage). A position is marked continuously to the mark price — the internal 8-hour order-book EMA by default, or the external anchor during a convergence window; if account equity — posted margin plus unrealised PnL — falls below the maintenance-margin requirement, it is liquidated. Deep, natural two-sided liquidity keeps liquidations orderly rather than toxic.

Risk Factors

  • Derivative exposure. The future confers no equity, voting, or economic interest in the issuer; per-share economics are fixed only at the prospectus rebase.
  • Leverage & liquidation. Adverse valuation moves are amplified by leverage; posted margin may be lost in full.
  • Settlement & event risk. A delayed, withdrawn, acquired, or renegotiated outcome changes the timing and basis of settlement, per the fallback logic.
  • Valuation-firm risk. A > 24-month delay routes settlement to independent NAV appraisals, which are estimates.
  • Liquidity risk. Early markets may show wider spreads and higher impact until natural short depth accumulates.

FAQ

What does the contract price?
The company's total enterprise valuation at IPO — a single verifiable outcome — quoted in $1B points, not a per-share price.
How is the mark determined?
Internal by default, external by override. Normally the mark is an 8-hour EMA of the order book — the market's own estimate. When a hard external event (a verified funding round, qualifying secondary, or corporate action) is confirmed, the mark converges to that anchor over 24–48h and the internal EMA is set aside until convergence completes.
Why price total valuation instead of per-share?
A per-share contract breaks whenever a new round changes the share count. Total-valuation pricing is immune to interim dilution; only the prospectus converts to per-share units.
What happens at the IPO?
On the prospectus filing the contract rebases PnL-neutrally to per-share units, then cash-settles against the IPO first-day closing price.
What if the IPO is delayed, withdrawn, or the company is acquired?
A > 24-month delay converts to NAV settlement via three independent valuation firms; an acquisition settles at disclosed enterprise value; a direct listing uses the reference price; a withdrawn IPO voids the contract and returns initial margin.
Where does short liquidity come from?
Naturally — investors with locked long positions hedging real holdings, market-makers capturing funding yield, and VCs/founders proxy-hedging — rather than subsidised market-makers.
How much leverage is available?
Initial margin as low as 10% (up to 10×), on isolated margin.
Do I own any shares?
Not through the future itself — it is cash-settled. The real pre-IPO equity that backs the market 1:1 is held separately.
